I didn't expect to see the Riemann Hypothesis proved in my lifetime. Yesterday Levent Alpöge said that he and Claude had found a proof. The same argument, he says, proves the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis (GRH) for Dirichlet *L*-functions. All this in [four pages on his website](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf).

<!--more-->

I keep getting this little jolt. *Riemann*. Then I think about what I was hoping for when somebody finally proved it and I get embarrassingly sad.

## The proof I thought we'd get

People announce proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis all the time.[^masullo] I've had people email me about great mathematical discoveries they thought they'd made. I think they hoped a bloke with a blog would be a more sympathetic audience than the academic establishment. I can see why they'd try.

Alpöge already had my attention from his work on Navier–Stokes. What makes this feel different is Lean, the program that checks the logical steps of a formal proof. Within hours Andrew Sutherland had matched the results to the standard Lean statements of RH and GRH, confirmed that they use only the usual foundational axioms and run the proof through several independently implemented checkers. It would be much harder for a bug in a checker to explain all of those results.[^lean]

I used to picture something like Andrew Wiles at the blackboard in Cambridge in 1993. There was a gap in the proof he announced. He and Richard Taylor repaired it and the papers appeared in 1995. To go from an announcement to serious independent checks in a few hours would have seemed miraculous then. I think it is miraculous now. I still wanted the blackboard.[^wiles]

What we have is a tarball in fourteen parts, with about 175,000 Lean files inside. Alpöge says the AI-generated write-up ran to over 250,000 pages. The first theorem starts by explaining that a real number different from one half must be either bigger or smaller than one half. Fair enough. I have read enough AI slop this year to know exactly what sort of first page that is. Alpöge says he wants to understand the argument better himself and write it up properly. I want something I can sit down with and get stuck on.[^announcement]

## A proof I can almost follow

While I was working on Tuesday's post about my master's thesis, I asked Claude about Cloitre's sequence from my undergraduate thesis.[^thesis] The rule uses least common multiples and the terms seem to come out as ones and primes. For that pattern to hold, there has to be a prime of the form \\(kp-1\\) below \\(p^2\\) for every prime \\(p\\). A standard bound under GRH only guarantees one below \\(2p^2(\log p)^2\\).[^least-prime]

I asked what would happen if you threw the kind of resources OpenAI had used for Navier–Stokes at this little sequence. Claude thought you could get partial results and put the odds of a full proof in the “low single digits”. Even the AI wasn't particularly AI-pilled.

On Numberphile a month ago Terence Tao wondered if some combination of humans and AIs might make further progress on RH “in 2050 or something”. “Who knows?” he said.[^tao-outlook] It sounded reasonable to me. I thought somebody would have to invent a new kind of mathematics first. Poor Terry Tao. Every time one of these announcements lands we seem to want him to tell us what it means for his field and what it means for civilisation. I keep going back to him because he takes both questions seriously.

I'd been trying to get my head around the earlier quasi-Riemann Hypothesis breakthrough. RH puts all the nontrivial zeros of the zeta function on the line with real part \\(1/2\\). Before this autumn, the region we could clear of zeros to the left of \\(1\\) got thinner the higher up you went. OpenAI's September paper gave a fixed boundary of \\(7/8\\) at *every* height. A shorter October paper gave \\(11/12\\) by a route people have found easier to read. Both bounds also apply to Dirichlet \\(L\\)-functions.[^quasi]

Tao called the \\(7/8\\) result the biggest advance on RH in fifty years. Measured from \\(1\\) to \\(1/2\\), it's a quarter of the way there. Getting this method to go left of \\(3/4\\), he said, would take another extremely clever idea. He described the argument as “three or four existing ideas arranged in a very unusual way”. He doubted any one mathematician alive would have put them together. Put the right people in a room and maybe they'd get there by trial and error.[^tao-quasi]

I wanted to know what was so unlikely about it. With some help from Claude and ChatGPT I can make out about four moves in the \\(11/12\\) proof.[^guide]

1. **Turn the zeros into poles.** A zero of \\(L(s)\\) is a pole of \\(1/L(s)\\). Enough cancellation among the Möbius coefficients lets the reciprocal extend into the region you want to clear. The proof works over the Eisenstein integers, where the sixth roots of unity it needs are available. Factorisation identities bring the bound back to Dirichlet \\(L\\)-functions, including zeta.
2. **Put copies of your sum inside a bigger average.** Twist the Möbius sum by characters that take sixth roots of unity as values away from the primes where they vanish. When the index is a prime raised to the sixth power the twist becomes \\(1\\) except on multiples of that prime. So the mean square over all the twists contains a lot of near-copies of the sum you wanted to bound.
3. **Get a theta function out of the Möbius sum.** Poisson summation produces Gauss sums. An identity absorbs the Möbius sign into cubic Gauss sums, which turn out to be the Fourier coefficients of Kubota's cubic theta function. Now you can use a transformation that Dunn and Radziwiłł developed in their work on Patterson's conjecture.
4. **Use the quadratic large sieve.** After the theta transformation the character that remains is the cube of a sextic character, hence quadratic. The quadratic large sieve gives the sharper estimate the proof needs. A repeated Poisson argument shrinks the leftover sums until they can be bounded too. Working back through the mean square gives the saving behind \\(11/12\\).

I can just about follow that on paper. If I'd needed a quadratic large sieve estimate, I might have started with quadratic twists. Then the square indices that would give me back the original sum are exactly the ones the quadratic large sieve throws out because they aren't squarefree. Who thinks to start with *sixth* roots of unity to get around that?

The stronger \\(7/8\\) argument uses the same theta machinery and a trick with selected primes that makes the effect of a hypothetical zero add up across them. The separate Landau–Siegel zero proof is strange in its own way. An exceptional real zero of a quadratic Dirichlet \\(L\\)-function would force a large bias in how primes fall into residue classes. A polynomial interpolation argument turns that bias into large powers of many primes dividing the norm of a nonzero determinant. A direct estimate says the norm is too small to be that divisible.[^siegel]

I hear people grumble that maths has got too big for any one person to know. That's easy to say and it always felt a bit abstract to me. Here I can see it. The person who knows how to get a good bound for the distribution of zeta zeros and the person who knows exactly what a cubic theta function will do under a transformation have perfectly good reasons to be working on different things. Even if you have both of them in the same room, somebody has to think to try the sixth powers. A model can draw on work across those specialisms in a single attempt and try a great many ways of putting it together. I'd badly underestimated what that might get you.

Some of the mathematics I find most beautiful started with that kind of leap. Euler had written the zeta function as a product over the primes. Riemann took the function into the complex plane and found that its zeros could tell him about the distribution of those primes.[^riemann] Wiles proved the part of the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture that links semistable elliptic curves to modular forms. That settled Fermat's Last Theorem and gave mathematicians a connection they could put to work on other problems.[^wiles]

## The day after

Alpöge's note has a tantalising line about chaining theta lifts to do the job that multiplicity does in Stepanov's argument over function fields. I can read the sentence. I'd love to know how you get from there to a proof about the primes. How much of the mathematics was already there, scattered across papers and different people's working lives? How much did they have to invent along the way? The quasi-RH work makes me think we might already know more than anybody can put together in one head.[^announcement]

I started this blog because I wanted to understand the Riemann Hypothesis. I liked having this question sitting there, more or less forever, while I understood a little more of it every few years. I was never going to solve it. I can still miss it. Alpöge calls it the loss of “a principal guiding light”. He was actually trying to prove the thing and it did me some good to read that he felt it too.[^announcement]

I was happy to let AI help write up the thesis experiment. My first impulse this time was to write it by hand. I wanted to get down what yesterday felt like while I could still feel it. I keep going from thinking about all the things we might get to learn to wondering if we're going to do ourselves in with this stuff. And then I think about a way into the proof, and I'm giddy again.

There's a rule that the answer to a headline ending in a question mark is *no*. This time I think it's *yes*.

I thought I'd know how I felt about that.
