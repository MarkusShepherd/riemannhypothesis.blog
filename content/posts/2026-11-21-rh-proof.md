> I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis.

That's from the [announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf) Levent Alpöge posted yesterday – just days after I published my [article on AI doing mathematics]({{<ref "posts/2026-11-17-revisiting-my-masters-thesis-with-ai.md">}}).

Claimed proofs are posted all the time.[^masullo] Even I have received a few messages over the years from people who thought they had made some great mathematical discovery, and who somehow thought that I would understand it better than the "academic establishment" – perhaps because I write a blog instead of publishing in journals.

<!-- more -->

## Why is this one different?

For one thing, Alpöge has quite the track record by now, including attaching his name to the Navier–Stokes result I mentioned last time.

For another: [Lean]({{<ref "posts/2026-11-17-revisiting-my-masters-thesis-with-ai.md#formal-verification-with-lean">}})! [Several outside sources](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) have already compiled and verified the formal proof, using different implementations, which greatly reduces the likelihood of a bug.

The fact that we can be confident this is indeed a correct proof after a couple of hours is itself a stark contrast to, say, Andrew Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. There, we had to wait a couple of years until the proof was verified, including the dramatic turn of an actual gap in his first approach. Wiles announced his proof in Cambridge in June 1993. The gap turned up during refereeing, and it took him about a year, and the help of his former student Richard Taylor, to fix it.

So, has it fallen? The general rule of thumb is that the answer to any headline ending in a question mark is *no*.[^betteridge] But in this case, it looks like we really have a *yes* on our hands.

## A whole bag of mixed emotions

I'm feeling a whole bag of mixed emotions right now: euphoria, confusion, loss, curiosity, excitement about the future.

I didn't expect this announcement in my lifetime. And now AI has got there within a couple of years.

Just a month ago, in a [Numberphile interview called *No More Heroes*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bAnwMdfcMVw&t=958s), Terence Tao was asked whether the Riemann Hypothesis was going to fall soon. It "hasn't fallen, but it's been dented quite a bit", he said (more on that dent below). He was sure the AI companies had secretly devoted much more time to it than they'd disclosed, "and they couldn't touch it. I mean, something beyond current AI is needed." Maybe "some combination of humans and AIs in 2050 or something" would make further progress.

When I was working on my previous article, I also discussed my undergrad thesis on [prime-generating sequences]({{<ref "posts/2015-09-23-prime-generating-sequences.md">}}) with Claude. It pointed out that Cloitre's conjecture, one of the open questions in the thesis, implies that for every prime \\(p\\) there's a prime \\(q\equiv-1\pmod p\\) below \\(p^2\\) – and even GRH only gets you to about \\(p^2\log^2p\\). Given the resources OpenAI threw at Navier–Stokes, it put the odds of a full proof in the low single digits. Even AI wasn't AI-pilled enough to believe any of this was ripe! And yesterday's proof, which covers GRH too, doesn't settle it either: Cloitre's conjecture needs more than that.

I am excited about what this means for maths. But I do feel like we were deprived of that Wiles moment, when he could announce his proof from a blackboard to the mathematical community – and finish with "I think I'll stop here."

Instead, we now have to trust gigabytes of code. (Alpöge also produced 250,000 pages of AI slop, but mercifully didn't post them.)

So the loss is palpable. The loss of the guiding light[^guiding-light] that was always considered the holy grail of mathematics, maybe of all human knowledge: something firmly out of reach of current methods and knowledge. And the loss of "the old ways".

A confession: whilst I was happy to heavily rely on AI to help me write up the details of my previous AI experiment, I really had to do this one by hand. Maybe it's the last bastion of human understanding. Or rather, the last desperate clinging to it.

## Human understanding attached

The announcement just dropped, it's massive and there isn't even an attempt at a human-digestible write-up. So we couldn't possibly hope to go through any of this material on this very amateurish blog. Quite the admission for a blog called *Understanding the Riemann Hypothesis*.

But we can take a look at the previous milestones from this autumn – the \\(7/8\\), the \\(11/12\\) and the no-Siegel-zeros proofs – which do have human understanding attached.

A quick recap first. The Riemann Hypothesis says that all non-trivial zeros of the zeta function have real part \\(1/2\\). Since 1896, we've known that they all lie strictly inside the critical strip \\(0<\Re s<1\\); that's what it took to prove the prime number theorem. Beyond that, writing \\(s=\sigma+it\\), the zero-free regions we knew looked like de la Vallée Poussin's classical

\\[ \sigma>1-\frac{c}{\log t}, \\]

hugging the line \\(\Re s=1\\) ever more tightly the further up you go. Over the decades, people improved the logarithm a bit, but nobody could rule out zeros creeping arbitrarily close to \\(1\\). A fixed zero-free half-plane \\(\Re s>\theta\\) for *any* \\(\theta<1\\), a so-called *quasi-Riemann hypothesis*, was way out of reach.

Then, in early October, OpenAI announced exactly that: no zeros with real part greater than \\(7/8\\), formally verified in Lean. By the [symmetry of the functional equation]({{<ref "posts/2013-10-31-perfect-symmetry.md">}}), all non-trivial zeros therefore lie in the strip \\(1/8\le\Re s\le7/8\\). In Tao's words, "we're one quarter of the way to the Riemann Hypothesis" – although their method "cannot get beyond three quarters without another extremely clever idea". Alongside it came a second, shorter argument for the weaker bound \\(11/12\\). That's the one human experts actually sat down and read.[^siegel]

Terence Tao again. The poor fellow has become the public face of understanding what all these AI-for-maths advances mean, so he's heavily over-cited. But in this case it's for the very good reason that he undoubtedly is one of the smartest people on earth who can assess the impact of all those announcements. He's neither AI-pilled nor AI-doomed, but pragmatic and sharp-eyed. And above all, he's thoughtful enough beyond mathematics to philosophise about the societal impact, too.

In the same interview, he called OpenAI's result "the biggest advance on the subject in 50 years", with a very clever new technique:

> It's sort of a combination of […] three or four existing ideas arranged in a very unusual way. […] It's not something any single mathematician alive, I think, would have come up with. I think if you got the right people to work together, lock them in a room, they might have come up with this after lots of trial and error.

I was curious what that means, so I asked Claude and ChatGPT to take me through it. Roughly, the shorter \\(11/12\\) proof, which shares the key ideas, decomposes into four steps:

1. **The classical skeleton.** Remember [tossing the prime coin]({{<ref "posts/2014-10-12-tossing-the-prime-coin.md">}})? The Möbius function \\(\mu(n)\\) looks like a sequence of random coin tosses, and \\(\sum\mu(n)n^{-s}=1/\zeta(s)\\). A zero of \\(\zeta\\) is a pole of \\(1/\zeta\\). So if you can show enough cancellation in the Möbius sums, say \\(M(x)=O(x^{\theta+\varepsilon})\\) for the Mertens function, there can't be any zeros with real part greater than \\(\theta\\). For \\(\theta=1/2\\), that's the Riemann Hypothesis itself. All of this is textbook analytic number theory. The hard part, of course, is proving the cancellation.

2. **Hide the sum in a bigger family, many times over.** Instead of the ordinary integers, the proof works with the Eisenstein integers, the integers of the number field \\(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})\\). That costs nothing: the zeta function of that field is \\(\zeta(s)\\) times another \\(L\\)-function, so every zero of \\(\zeta\\) is a zero there, too. What you gain are the sixth roots of unity, and with them sixth-power residue symbols, the machinery behind cubic and sextic reciprocity. Twisting the Möbius sum by those symbols gives a whole family of sums \\(A_u\\). Now the trick: if \\(u=p^6\\) is the sixth power of a prime, the twist is trivial (except where \\(p\\) divides \\(n\\)), so \\(A_{p^6}\\) is essentially our original sum again. It hides in the family once for every prime small enough for its sixth power to fit in.

   Here's the numerology, as far as I understand it. Say the family has about \\(D\\) members, each a sum of about \\(D\\) terms. If they behave like random walks on average, each one has size about \\(\sqrt{D}\\), and their squares add up to about \\(D^2\\). Our original sum appears about \\(D^{1/6}\\) times in that total, once for every prime up to \\(D^{1/6}\\), so it can be at most about

   \\[ \sqrt{D^2/D^{1/6}}=D^{11/12}. \\]

   That's where the \\(11/12\\) comes from: it's the sixth power. The catch is that proving the "random walks on average" part needs a strong *large sieve* inequality for sixth-power or cubic characters, and the known ones are too weak. For cubic characters, [Alexander Dunn and Maksym Radziwiłł](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.07463) had even shown (assuming GRH) that the best known bound can't be improved. Dead end.

3. **Turn the Möbius function into a modular form.** Both AIs singled this out as the real spark. Expanding the squares and applying Poisson summation replaces the sixth-power characters with Gauss sums. Then a classical identity for Gauss sums absorbs the Möbius sign, and what's left are cubic Gauss sums. These are something very special: the Fourier coefficients of Kubota's cubic theta function, a so-called *metaplectic form*. That's a cousin of modular forms, with an enormous amount of symmetry. So the Möbius function, our prime coin, the very thing that resists any structure, gets traded for the coefficients of a modular form! The analytic heavy lifting is then done by a summation formula from that same 2021 paper by Dunn and Radziwiłł, which was really about Patterson's conjecture on the bias of cubic Gauss sums. These are obscure objects. Very few people work on them, and those who do care about Patterson's conjecture, not zero-free regions.

4. **From sextic to quadratic.** The theta function's symmetry, its reflection formula, turns the sixth-power twist into the *cube* of a sixth-power character. And a sixth root of unity cubed is just \\(\pm1\\): the character has become quadratic. For quadratic characters, Roger Heath-Brown's large sieve is essentially optimal, which is exactly what was missing in step 2. A recursion of Poisson steps that's guaranteed to stop after a fixed number of rounds closes the argument. Note the irony: the proof deliberately starts with a *more* complicated family, sixth-power twists, just to get access to a *simpler* estimate after the transformation. Starting with quadratic twists directly doesn't work.

(Mind you, this is my reading of the AIs' reading of expositions of an AI proof. Take the details with a pinch of salt.)

Once you know the trick, Radziwiłł reckons he can sketch the core argument on half a page. Half a page! Compare that to what we're looking at now.

So why had nobody found it? That's exactly what Tao considered so unusual: there are experts in all those techniques, but no working mathematician would have all of them in their tool belt. The metaplectic people don't work on zero-free regions, and the zero-free-region people don't use metaplectic theta functions. On top of that, the specialists believed their tools were too weak for the job. Radziwiłł himself said as much to Quanta. So nobody had any reason to look for this particular combination.

## No more universal geniuses

There's been long-standing moaning about how fractured mathematics has become. There are no more universal geniuses, just specialised experts with a deep knowledge of one field, who find it hard to communicate their ideas to colleagues, even when their areas of expertise are adjacent.

And some of the greatest breakthroughs came precisely from building bridges between those fields.

Wiles' proof of Fermat's Last Theorem was celebrated in public because it solved a centuries-old question. Within mathematics, it was celebrated much more for *how* he solved it: by proving the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture,[^modularity] now the modularity theorem, and thus building a bridge between the previously separate worlds of modular forms and elliptic curves.

Riemann's genius itself was translating the discrete world of prime numbers into the seemingly far away world of complex analysis, via the zeta function, which encodes all the primes in [Euler's product]({{<ref "posts/2013-09-29-in-the-beginning-there-was-eulers-formula.md">}}). That allowed us to tackle problems in number theory with an entirely different set of tools and methods.

This is where AI truly shines. It's not just an expert in one field. It has read *all* the literature, and it's an expert in *every* field. In a single chat about my undergrad thesis, Claude pulled in Linnik's theorem, GRH, the parity problem from sieve theory, Tao's work on Collatz and finite automata. That's what enabled OpenAI's model to come up with this unusual combination, which humans were unlikely to find.

Again, we don't know yet what kind of clever ideas and unusual combinations there are in the RH proof. The overwhelming consensus amongst human experts was that some entirely new techniques, maybe even new fields of mathematics, would have to be developed before RH would fall. If everything checks out, maybe humans *did* in fact come up with all the required building blocks already. Even the smartest humans just weren't able to keep all of them in their heads, so we were waiting for computers big enough to do that.

## Utopia or extinction?

I am even more torn than before. Do all those breakthroughs of the past years mean we're entering some kind of utopia where knowledge and progress are abundant? Or is this humanity driving towards its own extinction?

One thing's for sure: the world of mathematics, and maybe the world as a whole, won't ever be quite the same after yesterday's announcement…
